II. Read: The Other Witch Hunt

20 min · Read, then left page, middle

It’s the early 1950s. World War II is over, and the US and the Soviet Union are locked in the Cold War. Everyone is scared of the bomb, and a lot of Americans are convinced communists are hiding next door.

HUAC. The House Un-American Activities Committee was a group in Congress that called people in and asked one question: are you now, or have you ever been, a communist? In 1947 it went after Hollywood. Ten writers and directors refused to answer. They went to prison. (Senator Joseph McCarthy ran his own hunt over in the Senate. Same panic, different room. That’s why the era is called McCarthyism.)

The blacklist. You didn’t need a trial to lose everything. Studios kept lists of “suspicious” people and stopped hiring them. Hundreds of careers ended over a rumor, an old meeting, or a petition someone signed years earlier.

Naming names. Here’s the trap. HUAC offered a way out: admit you used to be involved, then name everyone else who was. Do that and you could go back to work. Refuse and you were done. In 1952 Miller’s friend, the director Elia Kazan, named names. That same year Miller drove to Salem to read the 1692 court records. The Crucible opened on Broadway in January 1953.

Miller’s turn. In 1956 HUAC subpoenaed Miller himself. He answered questions about his own past. When they told him to name other people, he refused. Congress found him in contempt in 1957. A court threw out the conviction in 1958.

So why Salem? Miller couldn’t stand up and say “HUAC is a witch hunt” without getting blacklisted. So he wrote about an actual witch hunt and let the audience do the math.
